
Use a comprehension when there's one source iterable, at most one condition, and a transformation short enough to read left-to-right without backtracking. Use a loop when the body does more than produce one value per item (logging, try/except, mutating something outside), when there are two or more filters that each deserve explaining, or when nesting goes past one level — a nested comprehension reads in the opposite order from nested loops, which trips almost everyone. Also use a loop when the thing you're building isn't one value per item: an accumulator, a counter, a running total. You met that twice tonight with dedupe and word_count, so write those down as your own examples rather than inventing new ones.

why is "readable" sometimes more important than "clever" or "short" code?
Because the cost of writing code and the cost of reading it are wildly asymmetric. You write a line once, in ten minutes, with the whole problem loaded in your head. It then gets read dozens of times — by you in three weeks with none of that context, by whoever debugs it at 2am, by a reviewer deciding whether to trust it. Short and clever optimizes the ten minutes and taxes everything after. "Clever" usually means the logic is implied by the construct rather than stated, so every future reader has to re-derive what you already knew. Correct-but-opaque code also hides bugs: if you can't tell at a glance what a line produces, you can't tell at a glance that it's wrong.
